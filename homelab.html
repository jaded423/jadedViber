Two mini-PCs, one flat network, and a lot of watchdogs.

A two-node Proxmox cluster in a home office: a laptop with its screen off and a workstation with a Xeon, three VMs, two containers, a Raspberry Pi doing DNS, and a fleet of devices that reach all of it by name from anywhere. It streams the movies, watches the porch, runs a local LLM, mirrors my repos, and serves sermons to my phone. The interesting part is what happens when a piece of it breaks.

What it is

Two Proxmox nodes in a home office. book5 is a Galaxy Book laptop with its screen switched off. tower is a ThinkStation with sixteen Xeon cores, 78 GB of ECC memory, and a Quadro handed whole to the media VM. A Raspberry Pi 2 does DNS and breaks cluster ties. Everything else is a guest.

Why it exists

To run the things I'd rather not rent: media, cameras, a local LLM, a git mirror, an ERP to learn on, and a network I can break on purpose. And to give the ops habits I use at work a home: one source of truth per fact, a runbook per host, a log that survives the crash.

What it taught me

Most of the value is in the failure modes. A silent hang taught me to make crashes loud. A lightning strike taught me to check which bridge a VM is actually on. A mirror that came home taught me that "offsite" is a fact about geography, not intent.

How I reach it

Every host runs the Tailscale client and gets a name, so the bare alias works from anywhere: a phone on cellular, the Pocket in a café. Three things complicate that, and each has its own path. A few boxes have no Tailscale of their own, so a Raspberry Pi on the LAN advertises the whole subnet as a route. One VM lives behind Mullvad with lockdown on, which walls the tailnet off, so it's reached by jumping through its host. And a second overlay, Twingate, stays scoped to the web UIs that only answer on the LAN.

The bare alias is the tailnet, the -local suffix is the LAN. Flipped in May; before that it was the other way round, and every roaming device carried a reverse tunnel.

Where the bytes live

tower owns the disks. VM disks sit on an SSD pool; the media, the camera recordings, the model files, and the whole-machine backups sit on a 4 TB spinning pool that tower exports to VM 101 over NFS across the 2.5 GbE bridge. The VM itself has one root disk and nothing local worth keeping, so a rebuild is a rebuild, not a recovery.

Code lives in three places on purpose: GitHub, a Gitea on VM 101, and the bare mirror on pi1. A weekly sweep pushes to all three.

What happens when tower hangs

In March a kernel upgrade started hanging tower silently every one to three days. No panic, no log line, just a box that stopped answering until someone walked over. The fix was less about preventing the hang than about making sure the next one was loud, short, and left evidence.

A hang used to mean a walk to the office. Now it means a panic, a reboot, a dump in pstore, and the last lines already sitting on the other node.

One small thing it serves

The lab's newest job is the least infrastructural: sermons. The Pocket transcribes a recording locally with Whisper and builds a follow-along page with the audio. A nightly job syncs the pages and the mp3 into a container on book5. A Raspberry Pi in front of it terminates TLS and proxies the request, so the page is on my phone anywhere, with nothing exposed to the internet. The kit has its own page.

The container was born on tower and moved to book5 two days later, after tower hung twice in one day. Correlation, not proof; the move is the experiment.

2026 so far

  1. January

    The media library moved from book5 to a ZFS pool on tower, exported over NFS. Plex has read from there since.

  2. February

    A new router collapsed two subnets into one flat network. Both nodes moved to 2.5 GbE at the same time.

  3. March – May

    A kernel upgrade started hanging tower every one to three days. In May the kernel was pinned, lockups were set to panic, and remote syslog plus pstore went in.

  4. May

    The SSH convention flipped: the bare alias is Tailscale now and the LAN is the fallback. pi1 was reflashed to DietPi. VM 101 was consolidated onto one disk.

  5. June 19

    Lightning. One Wi-Fi node died. The cable reroute revealed VM 101 had been on 1 GbE the whole time; merged onto the 2.5 G bridge for about a quarter more throughput. A week later a second router replaced the Deco.

  6. July

    tower hung once more on the pinned kernel with an empty pstore, so book5 got a watchdog that power-cycles tower's smart plug after three silent minutes.

  7. August

    The PC and pi1 came home from the office. The git mirror is no longer offsite; GitHub and Gitea are the offsite copies now.

  8. September

    pi-gw1, a Raspberry Pi 5, joined as a Tailscale subnet router. The Pocket replaced the Mac as daily driver. A sermons container was born on tower, tower hung twice in two days, and the container moved to book5. On the 23rd a GL.iNet Flint 3 became the router, the third this year, with the Deco demoted to an access point.

Every host has a page in a typed wiki in the homeLab repo, with the same rule the work projects follow: one fact, one home, everything else points. The recovery runbook for a downed host is a Claude skill that walks reachability, web UI, service health, watchdog logs, and kernel forensics in that order, because that is the order I kept doing it in by hand.

Built in conversation

None of this was designed in one sitting. It accreted, one outage at a time, through dialogue with Claude: what should happen when tower stops answering at 2am, which bridge a VM is really on, whether a mirror in the same building counts as a backup. The lab is where those habits got practiced before they went to work.
